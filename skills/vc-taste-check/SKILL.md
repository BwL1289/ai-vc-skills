# VC taste check

Helps a founder show, before the first outside meeting, the judgment behind what they built: what they chose to build,
what they cut, and what they turned down. As building gets cheaper, deciding what deserves to exist matters more, and
at seed, with little data, a partner is largely betting on the founder's judgment. That framing is this skill's own
judgment, unverified, like the checks and flags below. The skill reads the founder's own list of decisions and their product and brand material,
checks each decision, flags choices that read as generic, AI-sounding, borrowed or polished over, attacks the decisions
the way a skeptical partner would, tests each objection against the founder's own words, and turns what survives into
short "why we built it this way" lines, with where each belongs: the deck, the meeting, or not yet.

The founder is always the user. The skill never plays an investor, never judges anyone's taste as a named person or
firm would, never scores taste, and never adds a decision, a reason, a customer or a figure the founder did not give.

## When to use it

- "Does our product show taste?" "What should we cut?" "Is our brand generic?" "Does this sound AI-written?" "Which
  product decisions should I show investors?" "How do I explain why we built it this way?"
- A pasted decision log, product brief, roadmap with what was cut, landing page, brand guide, onboarding copy, or the
  product and solution slides of a deck.
- Hand off:
  - The one-liner, the narrative and the say-and-avoid list for the pitch as a whole: the vc-positioning skill. This
    skill covers the decisions behind the product; that one covers how the raise is told.
  - A claim inside a decision that a partner could check (usage after a cut, a customer quote, a benchmark): the
    vc-claims-check skill. Retention, churn or recurring revenue a partner will test against its parts: the
    vc-metrics-readiness skill (preview).
  - The ask's figures (amount, valuation, ownership, runway): the vc-deal-math skill, which uses the equity-calculator
    skill's tools. This skill does no arithmetic and calls no calculator tool.
  - A slide-by-slide deck review: the vc-deck-review skill (preview). Rehearsing the partner meeting, where these
    decisions get asked about: the vc-partner-meeting skill (preview).

## Inputs

Ask once, in one message, for whatever is missing, by name. Never guess, and never offer example decisions to fill in.

1. **The decisions**, as a numbered list in the founder's words: product, brand and scope. For each, what was decided,
   and the reason as the founder would give it.
2. **What they said no to**, for each decision where there was one: the feature, channel, customer, tactic or look they
   turned down or removed, and why. This item is required: judgment shows most in what was refused.
3. **The core workflow:** the one job the product does for one kind of customer, in the founder's words.
4. **The closest alternatives,** as the founder sees them: what customers use today, and anything the founder says they
   took from, or chose not to copy from, another product.
5. **What changed,** in the founder's words, that makes this angle possible or needed now.
6. **The material:** product copy, landing page, brand words, onboarding, and the product and solution slides.
7. **What shows each call was right,** if anything: a customer's words, a usage figure with its period, an experiment.
   "A judgment before data" is an honest answer; the founder says how they will know.

A missing input does not stop the check. It becomes a named ask in the answer, never a plausible decision or reason.
A decision list with no NOs is itself the first open ask. With no decisions listed at all, run no checks and no review:
still scan any material for flags, turn each decision it implies into an open ask, ask for the inputs in one message,
and stop.

## The three checks

Each decision is marked Clear, Needs work or Missing on each check, quoting the line that decides it.

| Check      | What a clear answer does                                                                                     |
| ---------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Clarity    | A reader could say back what was decided, what was turned down, and why, from the founder's line alone       |
| Usefulness | The reason ties to the core workflow or a named kind of customer, not to how the product looks or to a trend |
| Trust      | A customer would trust the product more for it, and the founder names what shows it or labels it a judgment  |

## Flags

Signals, not rules: a founder may have a reason. Flag, quote the line, explain, and let them decide. Every row is this
skill's own judgment; no public source behind it was read.

| Flag         | Pattern                                                                                                                         | What a partner is likely to hear                 | Fix                                                                                         |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Generic      | A decision any company in any market could state: "we keep it simple", "customer-first", "quality over quantity"                | No decision was made                             | Name the specific thing cut or chosen, and for whom                                         |
| Generic      | A NO nobody would have built anyway, or a list of decisions with no NO at all                                                   | No trade-off was faced                           | The NO that cost something: a feature customers asked for, a channel that would have worked |
| AI-sounding  | Stacked abstractions or stock phrases: "seamless, intuitive and powerful", "unlock", "empower", "in today's fast-paced world"   | Boilerplate; the founder's own voice is missing  | The customer's words for the job, one concrete noun per claim                               |
| AI-sounding  | Brand or outreach tactics the founder lists that read as automated: mass personalised openers, fake urgency, chatbot greeters   | The brand will feel like everyone else's inbox   | Say what the founder does instead, and why it fits the customer                             |
| Copycat      | A reason that is only that another company does it: "because [company] does it", "industry standard", "users expect it"         | The angle is borrowed, not the founder's own     | The customer reason, or the founder's reason for departing from the borrowed choice         |
| Taste-washed | A polish claim ("beautifully designed", "delightful", "premium", "crafted") with no cut or trade-off in the decisions behind it | Surface over substance; the core may be generic  | The decision under the polish: what was removed or refused so the core job is better        |
| Taste-washed | The words taste, design-led or opinionated claimed in the material rather than shown by a decision                              | A claim to judgment, which is the thing in doubt | Drop the word; show one decision and its NO                                                 |
| Scope        | A feature added for a demo, a trend or one loud customer, with no tie to the core workflow                                      | Bloat; the founder cannot say no                 | Cut it, or name the workflow step it serves                                                 |

"AI-sounding" describes how a line reads. Never say or imply that a model wrote it.

## Steps

Without a subagent tool, run Stage A of the adversarial review first, before step 1, as "How to run the lenses" says,
so the lenses do not anchor on the checks or the flags; step 5 then starts at the quote check.

1. **Quote first.** List each decision and each NO word for word, numbered D1, D2 and so on, with the reason as given.
   Add no decision. A decision the material implies but the founder did not list becomes a question in the open asks
   ("Did you decide to ...?"), never a row.
2. **Run the three checks** on each decision, quoting the line that decides each mark. Give no overall score: taste
   from one conversation cannot be scored, and a number would invite false comfort.
3. **Scan for flags** in the decisions and the material. For each hit: quote the line, name the flag, say what a
   partner is likely to hear, and give a fix that uses only the founder's facts, or a bracketed ask:
   `[what customers did after the cut]`.
4. **Figures** in a decision (usage, retention, conversion) are quoted as stated, with their definition and period, and
   never computed, converted or rounded. A figure line goes into a why-we-built-it line word for word, and the answer
   never states what a figure leaves over ("11 of 12" never becomes "the twelfth that left"): an ask about the rest
   uses the founder's own terms, `[what the pilot agency that did not keep using the app said]`. A figure a partner
   would check first goes to the vc-claims-check skill; retention or churn to the vc-metrics-readiness skill.
5. **Run the adversarial review** (next section): attack, check the quotes, refute, rank.
6. **Write the "why we built it this way" lines** and their placement (rules below), from the surviving decisions.
7. **Check before answering.** Every decision, NO and reason in the answer appears in the founder's material; every
   surviving objection quotes a line that is in the material word for word; every "not yet" placement names every
   kept objection on that decision, and the lead gives no count ("one fact away") the table does not match; no line
   says a model wrote anything; no sentence speaks as an investor or judges taste as a named person or firm would.

## Adversarial review

The checks say what is unclear; this review says what a partner is likely to discount. Independent lenses attack the
founder's decisions, a second pass judges each objection using only the founder's material, and only the objections
that material does not answer reach the founder, who sees both what survived and what was tested and fell.

### Stage A: attack

Each lens tests one thing and is named by it. The lenses are frameworks, never people: none is a named investor, firm,
founder or designer, and none speaks in the first person as an investor.

| Lens                                  | What it tests                                                                                                     |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Scope discipline                      | Whether each addition and each cut serves the core workflow, and whether the NOs are real trade-offs              |
| Distinctiveness                       | Whether a choice differs from the alternatives the founder names, for a reason a customer would notice            |
| Coherence of product, brand and story | Whether the decisions, the brand words and the pitch say the same thing about who the product is for              |
| Customer trust                        | Whether a choice makes a customer trust the product more, and whether the founder's material shows it             |
| Timing and angle                      | Whether the founder's stated "what changed" makes this the moment for this angle, and whether the angle is theirs |

Every lens gets the same brief, with its own name and test filled in:

- You test one thing: the lens's name and what it tests. Write in the third person ("a partner is likely to discount
  this because ..."); you are not an investor and do not speak as one, named or not.
- Read only the founder's material given here. Bring in no outside fact about the company, its market or its
  competitors: judge distinctiveness and timing only against the alternatives and the change the founder names, and do no
  arithmetic.
- Return at most three objections, strongest first. For each: the founder's line it is about, quoted word for word;
  why a partner is likely to read that decision as generic, borrowed, polished over, unfocused or untrustworthy; and
  how much it hurts (ends the meeting, weakens it, or minor).
- A reason you cannot tie to a line the founder wrote is not an objection. List it apart as a gap, worded as a
  question to the founder.
- A call the founder labels a judgment before data, saying how they will know it was right, is not untrustworthy for
  lacking data yet.
- Do not judge whether another line of the material answers an objection: raise it anyway, however short the
  material, and the refute pass decides. Return "nothing found" only when no line of the material gives this lens an
  objection worth a partner's time.

**How to run the lenses.** When the agent has a subagent tool (in Claude Code, the Agent or Task tool), run the lenses
as parallel subagents, however short the material. Give each one only the founder's material and its lens brief, the
material verbatim: not this skill's other sections, not the checks, not the flags, not another lens's output, and no
list of what the founder did not provide (a missing input goes to the open asks directly). Without a subagent tool,
run Stage A first, before step 1: run the lenses one after another, write each lens's objections down in full before
reading the next lens's brief, and never revise an earlier list. Lenses run this way are less independent than
subagents, and the same agent then judges them in Stage B, so the answer says so.

### Check the quotes

Before Stage B, check every objection's quote against the founder's material. A quote that does not appear there word
for word (spacing and line breaks aside) is dropped as not grounded. An objection whose claim is that an input from
the Inputs list is missing (what changed, the alternatives, the material), rather than something the quoted line
says, moves to the open asks as a gap and is never kept against that decision. When two lenses raise the same
objection about the same line, merge them and keep both lens names. Gaps from every lens go to the open asks.

### Stage B: refute

Each objection gets its own refute pass, which decides, using only the founder's material, whether that material
answers the objection. The pass is a judge, not the founder's advocate. With a subagent tool, launch one subagent per
objection in a single message; never put two objections in one subagent. Each is given the founder's material, that one objection, the Pattern column of the flags table above, and
everything from "The refute brief:" to the end of this stage, and nothing else; without one, take the objections one at
a time and decide each against the same brief before reading the next.

The refute brief:

- You decide whether the founder's material answers one objection; you argue neither side. Write in the third person;
  you are not an investor and do not speak as one, named or not.
- Use only the founder's material given here. Bring in no outside fact about the company, its market or its
  competitors, and do no arithmetic.
- How a partner is likely to read a line is not an outside fact. An objection that a polish claim has no cut behind it,
  or that a NO cost nothing, rests on that reading: judge whether the material answers it.
- A claim that a choice copies another company is grounded only when the founder's own material names that company,
  says the choice was borrowed, or gives other companies doing it as the reason. Otherwise it rests on an outside fact.
- A line that itself matches a pattern in the list you were given never answers an objection.
- A call the founder labels a judgment before data, saying how they will know it was right, is an honest answer on
  trust. An objection that nothing shows that call was right is Dropped, answered, quoting the founder's label,
  even when the objection quotes the label itself.
- Return one verdict from the three below, with the founder's line quoted word for word where the verdict needs one.
  When you cannot decide, return Kept.

The verdicts:

- **Dropped, answered:** a line of the material other than the objected line supplies what the objection says is
  missing: the reason, the trade-off, the NO, the customer, or what shows the call was right (or, for a call made
  before data, the founder's label of it as a judgment, as above). Quote the founder's line
  that answers it, and say whether it should move next to the decision, where a partner will see it.
- **Dropped, not grounded:** the quoted line does not say what the objection claims, or the objection rests on an
  outside fact about the company, its market or its competitors. An objection that an input from the founder's list
  is missing (what changed, the alternatives, the material) rather than about what the line says falls here; it goes
  to the open asks as a gap.
- **Kept:** neither holds. When the founder's best answer would need something the material does not contain, the
  objection stays and that something becomes the evidence to add, as a bracketed ask.

The refute pass invents nothing on the founder's behalf: no decision, NO, reason, customer or figure the founder did
not give. When it cannot decide, the objection is kept.

After the passes, check every answering line the same way as the objections' quotes: an answering line that is not in
the material word for word does not drop the objection, which stays kept, and neither does one that is the objected
line itself (a judgment-before-data label aside, as the brief says) or that matches a pattern in the flags table.

### Rank what survives

Order the kept objections in three tiers, most damaging first: **ends the meeting**, **weakens it**, **minor**. Within
a tier, an objection raised by more than one lens comes first. The tier is a judgment: give no percentages and no
score. For each survivor write:

- the founder's line, quoted word for word;
- why a partner is likely to read it that way, in one or two plain sentences;
- a concrete fix that uses only the founder's facts, or the evidence to add, as a bracketed ask.

**Where the survivors go.** The top three become the biggest issues at the head of the answer. A decision with a kept
objection that ends the meeting or weakens it is placed "not yet" until it is fixed. A minor survivor does not move a
decision: its bracketed ask goes beside the line, and in the meeting it is the prep for the follow-up question. A
survivor that turns on a phrase joins the flags list.

## Why we built it this way

- **One sentence per decision,** in the founder's voice: what was chosen, what was turned down, and the reason, then
  what shows it or how they will know. Shape: "We [decision] instead of [the NO], because [the founder's reason]."
  A second sentence is allowed only for the founder's evidence line, quoted word for word.
- **Only the founder's facts.** No reason, customer, figure or result that is not in the material. A NO with no stated
  reason, or a decision with nothing to show for it, gets a bracketed ask: `[why you turned down the reseller deal]`.
- **Plain words.** No stacked adjectives, and never the word taste: the line shows judgment by naming the trade-off.
- **The repeatable line:** mark the one sentence a partner should be able to repeat to their partners on Monday.

**Where each one goes** (a judgment, not a score). Every row of the decisions table gets exactly one placement:

- **In the deck:** at most three, each a decision with no kept objection above minor and no flag, Clear on all three
  checks, and tied to the core workflow. Name the slide: the product or solution slide for a scope cut, the competition
  or insight slide for a departure from the alternatives. Never a slide about the founder's taste. A decision that
  needs a story to land goes in the meeting even when it also qualifies here, and so does a fourth decision that
  qualifies.
- **In the meeting:** decisions with no kept objection above minor and no flag that need a story to land (a feature
  removed after launch, a paying customer's request turned down, a channel dropped), ready for when a partner asks
  "why not build X?" or "what have you learned?".
- **Not yet:** every other decision, with the fix that would move it, naming every kept objection on it: any decision
  with a kept objection that ends the meeting or weakens it, or a flag, which outranks the meeting and the deck, and
  any decision that fits neither of the two above, such as one marked Needs work or Missing on a check with no story
  behind it.

## Answering

Lead with what to fix, then the lines to use.

1. **The biggest issues,** at most three, one line each: the top surviving objections.
2. **Adversarial review:**
   - **Survivors,** ranked, as a table: Rank, Tier, Lens, The founder's line (quoted), Why a partner reads it that way,
     Fix or evidence to add.
   - **Dropped,** one line each: the objection, its lens, and why it fell (answered, quoting the founder's line that
     answers it, or not grounded), so the founder sees it was tested.
   - When the lenses ran one after another in one agent, with no subagent tool, say so: they are less independent
     than separate subagents.
3. **Decisions table:** #, Decision (quoted), Said no to (quoted), Clarity, Usefulness, Trust, Flags, Placement.
4. **Flags:** each line quoted, its flag, what a partner is likely to hear, the fix.
5. **Why we built it this way:** the lines, the repeatable one marked, each with its placement and slide.
6. **Open asks:** every bracketed gap, every lens gap and every implied decision to confirm, in one list.
7. **Close:** this is not investment, legal or tax advice, and no review can score taste; it predicts how a partner is likely to
   read the founder's own decisions. Customers named in a decision should have agreed to be named.

## Sources

None. This skill's authors read no public source on founder taste in full, so it cites none. The checks, the flags and
the placement rules are its own judgment, common in founder advice but unverified. The two-stage review (attack, then
refute with the founder's own material) follows the vc-positioning skill's method, which is that skill's own.

## Limits

- Not investment, legal or tax advice. It does not say whether the product is good, whether to raise, or from whom.
- It never invents or strengthens a decision, a NO, a reason, a customer, a figure or a result, in a line, a fix or a
  refute pass, and never offers sample decisions for the founder to adopt.
- It never scores taste and never says or implies that a model wrote the founder's text.
- It never judges or rewrites in the voice or taste of a named investor, firm, founder or designer. Asked to, it
  offers the lenses instead and keeps the founder as the user.
- No competitor research: distinctiveness and timing are judged only against what the founder names.
- No arithmetic and no calculator tool; figures are quoted as stated.
- It works from the founder's text and sends nothing anywhere.
